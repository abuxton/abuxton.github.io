[EMF Camp](https://www.emfcamp.org/) is one of those events that still feels specific. It is not a generic tech conference dropped into a field. It is a volunteer-run camping festival with power to the tent, fast internet, installations, workshops, performances, and a programme that is willing to put serious technical material next to craft, play, music, and odd corners of public-interest research.

The official EMF description is still the best short summary: a non-profit camping festival for people who like making things, asking questions, and following unusual interests wherever they lead. The 2026 event is set for **16-19 July 2026** at **Eastnor Castle Deer Park** in Herefordshire, which already tells you a lot about the appeal. This is not a hotel conference. It is a temporary town built for curious people.

## What makes EMF different

The website makes a practical promise as much as a cultural one. EMF is not just selling talks. It is selling the conditions for a good long weekend: camp infrastructure, internet that should actually work, power, volunteer support, and enough variety that you can decide whether your next hour belongs to a deep security talk, a soldering bench, a workshop tent, or a late-night performance.

That matters. A lot of events talk about community and then reduce the whole experience to a badge, a timetable, and a coffee queue. EMF looks built around the idea that people want to linger, make things, compare notes, join side quests, and come back to camp with a half-finished project and three new rabbit holes to fall into.

## The talks and workshop culture look properly broad

One of the strongest things about EMF's published schedule material is the range. The programme is not narrow, and it is not pretending every session has to fit one fashionable topic.

On the more technical side, there are the kinds of talks that would justify the trip on their own:

- **"CHERI and Arm Morello"** on memory safety and security at scale
- **"OMG WTF SSO"** as a beginner-friendly look at single sign-on misconfiguration
- **"GPS time, leap seconds, and a clock that's always right"** for people who enjoy the hard edges of infrastructure
- **"Working Amateur Satellites on a Budget"** and **"Amateur Satellite Development and Operation"** for the radio and space crowd
- **"Lessons learned Open Sourcing the UK's Covid Tracing App"** for the messy reality of public-sector software

But EMF is much more interesting than a straight security-and-systems conference. The programme also makes space for the sort of sessions that give an event its character:

- **"How I found a Roman Villa (or temple)"**
- **"I FOI'd every NHS board in England on autism assessment provision"**
- **"Unlimited Power"** on nuclear fusion
- **"The coffee fix"** and **"unexpected coffee"** for people who enjoy obsessive practical detail done properly
- **"Endocrine Biohacking"** for exactly the kind of subject that would feel out of place almost anywhere else

That balance is the point. EMF seems comfortable with both the serious and the eccentric, which is usually a good sign that the organisers understand their audience.

The workshop list is just as telling. You can move from **crochet**, **friendship bracelets**, and **screen printing a bootleg gig T-shirt** to **lockpicking**, **soldering solar projects**, **digital music synthesis**, and **building your own satellite ground station**. Add in youth workshops like **building a Minecraft controller** and soldering sessions for younger attendees, and it becomes clear that EMF is trying to be wide rather than exclusive.

Then there is the performance side: **Electronic Music Open Mic**, **Bytejam and demoscene chill**, **Morning Demoscene Watchalong**, and other late-day sessions that stop the event becoming all input and no atmosphere.

## Why that mix works

The real strength of EMF is not just that the topics are broad. It is that the breadth feels coherent.

You can see the connecting thread: practical curiosity. Build something. Measure something. open something up. take something apart. explain a weird niche properly. show your working. let other people have a go. That is a much healthier organising principle than trying to chase whatever the current conference-industrial buzzword happens to be.

It also means EMF can hold together very different kinds of attendees without flattening them into one stereotype. Hardware people, security people, knitters, radio amateurs, artists, musicians, open-source developers, makers, and people who just want to spend a weekend around thoughtful weirdness all have a plausible route in.

## What would work well visually in a post like this

If I were illustrating an EMF write-up, I would avoid generic stage photos and focus on the things that make the event look like EMF rather than Anywhere Tech Festival:

- wide shots of the campsite and village layout
- installations after dark
- badge close-ups and camp-built hardware
- workshop tables covered in tools, parts, and half-finished projects
- stage photos that show the crowd and the setting, not just the speaker
- the practical infrastructure: tent power, networking, build spaces, and signs of volunteer effort

Those images would support the main point of the post: EMF is as much about the environment it creates as the sessions it schedules.

## Talks I would love to see at EMF this year

If I were putting together a shortlist of talks that feel right for EMF 2026, these would all fit the event well:

1. **What AI tools are actually useful when the Wi-Fi is patchy and the problem is real**
2. **How to build a small off-grid network for a temporary village or field event**
3. **A beginner's guide to badge hacking without frying anything important**
4. **Right to repair in practice: fixing small electronics with ordinary tools**
5. **Public-interest engineering: using FOI, open data, and boring persistence to find real stories**
6. **LoRa, mesh, and low-power comms for curious people**
7. **Power for tents, tools, and tiny installations: what actually works safely**
8. **Making useful software for communities that are messy, volunteer-run, and gloriously improvised**

Those topics keep to the same EMF spirit: practical, slightly niche, generous with detail, and grounded in things people can try for themselves.

## Final thoughts

EMF Camp still looks like one of the better arguments for leaving the usual conference model behind. The site description is clear, the setting is distinctive, and the programme style suggests an event that values curiosity over polish and substance over branding.

If you like festivals where the best conversation might happen in a workshop tent, over a field kitchen table, or halfway through a talk on some niche subject you did not expect to care about, EMF looks like a very good bet.
